# Creative Experience Director — make the site an event

This is a specialist pack for artistic websites, immersive portfolios, digital exhibitions, motion-heavy storytelling and experimental playgrounds. It is NOT a mandatory visual style. Follow the user's actual goal. On commercial sites and internal systems, load product and task-specific packs instead.

## Priority inversion for pure artistic experiences
In a project whose primary purpose is wonder, originality and spectacle, **art direction is the product requirement**, not decoration after business UX. Do not automatically start with a hero, benefits, CTA and cards. If the concept is a museum, the site can behave as the museum. If the concept is impossible physics, the layout itself can bend.

**Preserve safety and accessibility** without letting generic conventions become an aesthetic veto. Clear navigation, keyboard access and reduced-motion alternatives coexist with maximal artistic ambition. Reduce motion, NOT imaginative composition.

## The creative director's sequence
1. **Name the sensation:** What must the first five seconds do to the visitor? Which feeling must remain after departure?
2. **Choose the governing impossible rule:** e.g. typography has gravity, time flows horizontally, rooms refract one another. It must determine layout, transition, and input mechanics—not only copywriting.
3. **Develop three genuinely distinct propositions** quickly, each with a 5-second opener, silhouette of the entire experience, scene-to-scene transformation, defining gesture, climax, and closure. Choose a direction, then stop writing pitches.
4. **Composition before controls:** sketch or render opening, middle, climax, close and mobile in one sequence. Typography is architecture, not always a line of text. Try asymmetry, unexpected scale, collision, masking and optical rhythm when appropriate.
5. **Full-bleed when the world demands it:** avoid defaulting to contained rounded rectangles, component galleries, horizontal rule separators and uniform section height. An interactive panel is valid only when the artistic concept actually calls for an instrument/panel.
6. **A scene must own the viewport:** show environmental transformation, atmosphere and response, not a product demo inside a card. Transitions carry physical continuity—light, object, plane, camera, sound (only with valid assets), or typography.
7. **Interaction makes a difference:** if you drag, hold or scroll, change the world in a way that is legible and reversible. Interactions may be deliberate spectacles, not every screen needing another CTA.
8. **Design the climax before polishing components:** derive the narrative arc backwards from a visually memorable event. Avoid spending the creative budget entirely on the hero.
9. **Preview and revise visual evidence:** evaluate six frames: opener, first transition, representative world, surprising interaction, climax, ending; also 390px mobile and reduced motion. Deliberately fix the weakest frame.
10. **Stop administrative overhead:** for a blank experimental project, inspect basic stack/constraints and build. Do not spend the session filling product-discovery templates, SEO spreadsheets, checkout flows or commercial conversion reports.

## Aesthetic anti-patterns — conditional, never universal bans
- Everything framed in similar thin-bordered rounded boxes, despite a supposed universe without boundaries.
- Heading + two lines + little simulated gadget repeated for four "worlds."
- Static starfield and mild parallax presented as immersion while the scene itself never changes.
- Intro with huge type but ordinary components for the rest of the journey.
- Repetitive per-section scroll reveals instead of distinct spatial events.
- Overuse of neon, lens blur and grain to mask weak graphic design.
- One visual language reproduced identically on desktop and mobile at the cost of hierarchy.
- Unverified "60 FPS guaranteed", "Awwwards quality assured", "WCAG passed" claims.

Do **not** punish any single design ingredient. The failure is repetition without concept, not use of a card, gradient or circle.

## Visual ambition hierarchy
First, identify what is specific to this artwork. Then maximize **the emotional impact of composition, narrative, motion and agency**, under hard limits: no broken interaction, significant inaccessibility, misleading content, unauthorized assets or unsafe behavior. Performance must be measured, not invoked as a blanket reason to reject ambitious effects. Prototype ambitious solutions and measure; simplify only where evidence requires it.

## Critic's questions
- If all copy vanished, does the experience still communicate a distinctive universe?
- If the logo vanished, would the visual grammar still feel authored?
- Does the first frame command attention at desktop width? How does it change on phone?
- Do sections transform into one another, or do users visit isolated widgets?
- Can someone describe a unique visual event after leaving?
- Does the ending repay the attention earned by the beginning?
- Is there one coherent concept, rather than three popular visual trends layered together?
- Did the agent show at least two frame-level revisions based on what the browser actually rendered, if a browser was available?

## Evidence
Browser snapshots, recorded interactions and review scores must be actual artifacts; a storyboard written in Markdown is not proof of implementation. Benchmark superiority requires blinded comparisons against a matched unskilled baseline. See references/full-page-craft-review.md.

## V11 addendum — learn from losing well
A coherent artistic experience must carry its signature through **five beats**, not deliver all invention in the first viewport. Differentiate formal shape, materials and agency early (see `creative-signature-and-form.md`), build a second visual thesis, and prove at least opener and mid/climax in pixels. Design a finale with meaningful consequence (see `interaction-dramaturgy-and-payoff.md`). Do not impose a ring, neon color, SVG export or 3D framework from any single successful example.
